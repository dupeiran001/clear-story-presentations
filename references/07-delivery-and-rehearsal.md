# 07 — Spoken delivery, speaker notes and rehearsal

**Presenting is not narrating a PDF.** The slide gives visual reference; the speaker directs attention and reasoning. Let listeners listen without reading a wall of on-screen text.

## Speaker-note schema

For each slide write:

`Purpose | opening spoken line | what to point to | essential explanation | interpretation | transition | time | caveat / backup reference`

Speaker notes should use idiomatic spoken language. Technical terms are fine when audience-specific and introduced. Do not script the exact visible text a second time.

## Opening and signposting

Within the opening, establish (in the order best for the audience): what question matters, why now, what the audience will get, and how to navigate the argument. Skip résumé slides and breathless generalities unless speaker identity is directly relevant. Use a preview for long complex talks; for 3-minute briefings, answer-first may be enough.

Signposts should correspond to **reasoning**: “We know the system fails at peak time. The next question is why.” Avoid “Moving on to the next slide.”

## Natural spoken rhythm

- Alternate short declarative sentences with enough explanation for a difficult point.
- Give listeners a moment to inspect a new figure before interpreting it.
- Say numbers with context, not strings of significant digits.
- Repeat the **main message**, not whole sentences or every metric.
- Use controlled pauses at genuine inflection points.
- Avoid reading chart legend labels after the audience has already seen them; explain relationships.
- Anchor gestures/laser pointer on a bounded area, not aimlessly roaming.

## Timing plan

Budget by **concept difficulty and demonstration behavior**, not by fixed slides/minute. Suggested budgeting procedure:

1. Reserve time for introduction, transition overhead, and conclusion.
2. Separate Q&A from speech time; include demo contingency if applicable.
3. Estimate each slide's spoken words and visual parsing time.
4. Rehearse at natural speed with actual visuals and click/build timing.
5. Cut secondary points if runtime exceeds target; do not solve by speaking unnaturally fast.

For live demos, prepare a fallback screenshot/recording and rehearse failure narrative (“The result would normally appear here; the saved output demonstrates …”), with limitations honestly stated.

## Q&A design

Prepare questions in layers:

- **Clarity:** define terms, recap mechanism.
- **Evidence:** source, baseline, sample, assumptions.
- **Challenge:** alternatives, counterexamples, known limits.
- **Implementation:** cost, risk, owner, timeline, reproducibility.
- **Implication:** who should act or what further test matters.

Use backup slides when likely questions demand detail. Acknowledge unknowns explicitly. Repeat a question only when it benefits the room; don't use rote filler while thinking.

## Rehearsal review

Record or practice in an environment similar to delivery, if permitted. Ask a representative listener to answer:

- What was the one main point?
- Which slide was hardest to follow, and why?
- What did you infer that the presenter didn't explicitly say?
- What evidence convinced you, and what didn't?
- What will you do differently after this talk?

Revise weak inference steps before editing animation. If participants recall only the visual theme but not the message, the deck failed.
